    Module 1

    What AI is, and its kinds

    EveryoneTechnical
    Learn

    "AI" is a big umbrella. It covers any system that does work that usually takes human judgment: spotting, predicting, deciding, creating. Generative AI is one branch of it.

    Two quick distinctions. Predictive AI sorts or forecasts (is this spam?). Generative AI makes something new (draft a reply). And everything you can use today is narrow, built for specific jobs. AI that matches people at everything is still a debated idea.

    See
    KindHow it worksStrengthLimit
    SymbolicPeople write explicit if-then rules and logicEvery step can be explainedBreaks on cases the rules didn't foresee
    Machine learningLearns from examples: labeled (supervised), unlabeled (unsupervised), or by trial and error (reinforcement)Handles messy, real-world patternsOnly as good as its data; harder to explain
    Deep learningMachine learning with many-layered neural networksImages, speech, languageNeeds lots of data and computing power
    Generative AIDeep learning that produces new text, images, audio or codeFlexible, works from plain languageCan produce fluent content that is false
    Try
    Sort these everyday technologies. Pick the most specific kind.

    Kinds overlap: deep learning is a kind of machine learning, so pick the innermost box that fits.

    No account needed for this one.

    Your call

    When someone says "AI," ask which kind. You can be fine with a spam filter and still pass on chatbots, or the other way around.

    Module 2

    How large language models work

    EveryoneTechnical
    Learn

    A large language model (LLM) guesses the next chunk of text, again and again, using patterns from a huge pile of writing. It doesn't look anything up unless it has a search tool, and nothing in that loop checks whether it's true.

    TermPlain meaning
    TokenA chunk of text, often part of a word. Models read and write in tokens.
    TrainingThe one-time process where the model learns patterns. Its knowledge stops at a cutoff date.
    Context windowEverything the model can see in one session: your messages, files, and its own replies.
    See
    The library opens at

    Illustrative numbers chosen to show the idea, not output from a real model. A model picks a likely word, not a checked one.

    Try

    Write three words that could come after "The library opens at." Rank them. That's roughly what the model does for every word. The likeliest guess isn't always right for Brandeis.

    No-account version: try it with a sentence from your own field. Where would a guess go wrong?

    Example
    1. Open any chat tool.
    2. Paste the prompt below and read the three endings. Each is plausible. None is checked.
    3. Then ask for today's actual library hours. Without a search tool, it guesses. With one, it should cite a page you can open.
    Finish this sentence three different ways, then tell me which one
is most likely and why: "The library opens at"
    Your call

    If the facts need to be exact and current, use it only when you can check every claim.

    Module 7

    Running AI on your own computer

    SkepticalResearchersEthicalPractical
    Learn

    You can download some models (called open-weight) and run them right on your laptop. A local model needs no account, works offline, and keeps your prompts on your machine. The catch: they're smaller, they need memory, and they still make things up.

    Most laptops run a compressed version (quantization, like 4-bit). Smaller file, slightly lower quality.

    See
    Your memory (GPU, or unified memory on Apple)Model size that fits (4-bit)Examples
    4 to 6 GB, or no graphics card1 to 4 billion parametersQwen3 4B, Gemma 3 4B, Phi-4-mini
    8 GB7 to 8 billionLlama 3.1 8B, Qwen3 8B
    12 to 16 GB13 to 14 billionQwen3 14B
    24 GB or moreabout 30 billionQwen3 32B

    Source: llmhardware.io, How to Run LLMs Locally (2026). It reports roughly 30 to 80 tokens per second on a graphics card and 3 to 8 on a processor alone.

    Local modelCloud assistant
    Where prompts goStay on your computerSent to the provider
    Account neededNoUsually
    Works offlineYesNo
    Cost per useYour own electricityFree tier or subscription
    CapabilitySmaller models, fewer toolsLarger models, search, files, agents
    Invented factsYesYes
    Try

    Find your computer's memory (Mac: Apple menu, About This Mac. Windows: Settings, System, About). Which row are you?

    No-download version: find your row and decide whether a local model would help you at all.

    Example

    Run a small model, no account needed. Check your department's software rules first.

    1. Install Ollama (command line) or LM Studio (a desktop app with a chat window).
    2. In Ollama, open Terminal and run the line below. It downloads about 3 GB once, then starts a chat. In LM Studio, search for the same model and click Download.
    ollama run qwen3:4b
    1. Turn off Wi-Fi.
    2. Ask it to summarize a paragraph you paste in. It still answers, which shows it runs without the internet.
    3. Ask it a factual question about Brandeis. Compare its answer with a source. Smaller models guess more often.
    4. Type /bye to exit. Delete the model later with ollama rm qwen3:4b to free the space.
    Your call

    Local can be a good fit for private drafts, if your policies allow it. Check the model's license for work use, and remember local models still get things wrong.

    Module 8

    Build your own offline sandbox

    SkepticalResearchersEthicalTechnical
    Learn

    A sandbox is a closed space where you can experiment without anything leaking out. An offline sandbox puts a local model and a chat window on your own laptop, then cuts the internet. You can poke at it with real documents, and your files never leave your machine.

    It has three layers: the model (Ollama runs it), the interface (Open WebUI, a chat page in your browser), and the fence (Wi-Fi off, nothing open to the network).

    See
    Try

    Before you build anything, draw where your data goes. Sketch one box for a cloud chat tool and one for a sandbox, then trace a document from your desk to the answer in each.

    No download needed for this one.

    Example

    The easy path, no command line: install LM Studio, download a small model from inside it, turn off Wi-Fi, and chat. That's already a working sandbox.

    The fuller path, with document chat: Ollama plus Open WebUI (setup guide).

    1. Install Ollama and Docker Desktop.
    2. While you're still online, download a chat model and the model that reads documents:
    ollama pull llama3.2
ollama pull nomic-embed-text
    1. Start the chat page:
    docker run -d -p 3000:8080 \
  --add-host=host.docker.internal:host-gateway \
  -v open-webui:/app/backend/data \
  --name open-webui --restart always \
  ghcr.io/open-webui/open-webui:main
    1. Open http://localhost:3000 and create the first account. It lives only on your laptop.
    2. Turn off Wi-Fi. Practice with a made-up document first.
    3. Upload a file and ask about it. If answers still come with Wi-Fi off, the sandbox is sealed.
    4. When you're done, wipe it: docker rm -f open-webui and docker volume rm open-webui.
    Is my sandbox sealed?
    Your call

    Offline controls where your data goes. It doesn't make the answers accurate. Some data rules (IRB protocols, FERPA, data use agreements) only allow approved university machines, so check with IT or your IRB before using real records.

    Context and setting up a system
    
      
    UsersEducatorsStaffPractical
    
    
    Learn

    See
    
      
    
    
    
    Try

    Example
    
      
    Build a course FAQ assistant.
    
      
      
        
    1. Create a project, Gem or notebook in a Brandeis-provided tool.
      2. 
        
    2. Upload the syllabus and course policies. Leave out anything with student names.
      3. 
        
    3. Paste these instructions:
      4. 
      
    
      
    You answer questions about [COURSE NAME] using only the attached

- If the answer is not in the files, say "I don't see that in the

      2.

    Module 11
    Workflows
    
      
    StaffUsersPractical
    
    
    Learn
    
      
    A workflow is a set of steps you repeat, where AI does some and you do the rest. The savings come from the routine, and the best ones mark exactly where you check the work.
    
    
    
    
    See
    
      
    
    
    
    Try
    
      
    Write out one weekly task as steps. Label each Me, AI, or Me checks AI. Time it before and after, checking included.
    
      
    No-account version: write the steps, then mark the ones you'd never hand off, and why.
    
    
    
    
    Example
    
      
    A. Meeting notes to action items. Paste your notes (or a transcript everyone agreed to) with:
    
      
    From these notes, list every action item as: owner, task, due date.
If an owner or date is unclear, write "unclear" instead of guessing.
Then list open questions.
    
      
    B. Draft, critique, revise. You write the draft. The tool only critiques. The words stay yours.
    
      
    Act as a skeptical reviewer in [field]. List the three weakest
points in this draft and why. Do not rewrite it.
    
    
    
    
    Your call
    Keep any step that builds a skill you care about, or where a mistake would be costly and hard to catch.

    Module 12
    Agents and loop engineering
    
      
    Daily usersEducatorsTechnical
    
    
    Learn
    
      
    An agent is a model that can act (search, read files, run code) and pick its next step from what happened. "Loop engineering" covers the cycles around it that keep its work reliable (DeepLearning.AI). A person sets the terms for every loop. Tap one to see how.
    
    
    
    
    See
    
      
    
        
        
    
      
    
    
    
    
    Try
    
      
    Write a three-item pass/fail rubric for something you make often. That's a verification loop, done by hand.
    
      
    No-account version: use the rubric on your own work for a week. It helps with or without AI.
    
    
    
    
    Example
    
      
    A rubric checker, step by step.
    
      
      
        
    1. Write your rubric. For a workshop announcement: names a date and room; under 80 words; exactly one sign-up link.
      2. 
        
    2. Write or generate a draft.
      3. 
        
    3. In a new chat, paste:
      4. 
      
    
      
    Score this text against the rubric. For each item, answer PASS or
FAIL and quote the line that shows it. Do not rewrite the text.
Rubric:
1. Names a date and a room
2. Under 80 words
3. Has exactly one sign-up link
Text: <paste here>
    
      
      
        
    1. If anything fails, revise and run the checker again.
      2. 
        
    2. Do this on five drafts and note which item fails most. That record is a small learning loop.
      3. 
      
    
    
    
    
    Your call
    Event-driven loops run without asking you. Keep them away from sending, spending or changing records until you've watched them work.

    Module 13
    Agentic programming
    
      
    EducatorsResearchersTechnical
    
    
    Learn
    
      
    Coding agents (Claude Code, GitHub Copilot's agent mode, Cursor, OpenAI Codex) write and run code in a project folder. You describe it, and the agent plans, builds and tests. Your job becomes specifying clearly and reviewing carefully.
    
    
    
    
    See
    
      
    
    
    
    Try
    
      
    Write a one-page spec for a small tool you wish existed. Use the template below.
    
      
    No-account version: have a classmate build your spec on paper. Every question they ask is a gap an agent would have guessed at.
    
    
    
    
    Example
    
      
    Build a workshop sign-up tracker.
    
      
      
        
    1. Make an empty folder and open it in a coding agent.
      2. 
        
    2. Save this as SPEC.md:
      3. 
      
    
      
    # Workshop sign-up tracker
Goal: one web page where staff see who signed up for each workshop.
Must:
- Read sign-ups from signups.csv (name, email, workshop, date)
- Show one card per workshop with a count and a list of names
- Flag workshops over capacity (capacity is in workshops.csv)
Must not:
- Send any email
- Use any outside service or login
Done when: opening index.html shows the cards, and the
over-capacity workshop is highlighted.
    
      
      
        
    1. Prompt: Read SPEC.md. Propose a plan in five steps or fewer. Do not write code yet.
      2. 
        
    2. Read the plan. Remove anything you didn't ask for.
      3. 
        
    3. Prompt: Build step 1 only, then stop and show me what changed.
      4. 
        
    4. Review each change before approving the next step.
      5. 
        
    5. Test with fake data, never real student data.
      6. 
      
    
      
    Review checklist: Does it do only what the spec says? Can you explain each file? Did it add anything that connects to the internet? Do the tests check the "done when" line?
    
    
    
    
    Your call
    If you can't read or test the code, keep the tool small, offline and away from real data. Course rules come first.

    Module 14
    Teaching and policy
    
      
    EducatorsStudentsEthical
    
    
    Learn
    
      
    Brandeis asks every syllabus to state an AI policy and offers four templates (Brandeis CTL). All four are valid, and a course with zero AI is as legitimate as one built around it.
    
    
    
    
    See
    
      
    
        
    Most restrictiveMost permissive
    
        
    
        
    
      
    
    
    
    
    Try
    
      
    Pick the template closest to your course and tweak one sentence for your field.
    
      
    No account needed for this one.
    
    
    
    
    Example
    
      
    Conscientious objection. People may say no to AI for ethical, religious, environmental or professional reasons. If AI is required, offer an equal non-AI path that meets the same learning goal.
    
      
    Redesign one assignment, step by step.
    
      
      
        
    1. Write the assignment's learning goal in one sentence.
      2. 
        
    2. Ask: could a chat tool produce a passing answer? Decide whether that matters for this goal.
      3. 
        
    3. If it matters, add a step that shows process: draft history, an oral check-in, in-class work, or a reflection on choices.
      4. 
        
    4. If your policy allows AI, require a short note on how it was used.
      5. 
        
    5. Write the non-AI path, with the same learning goal.
      6. 
      
    
      
    Teaching agentic programming. Grade the spec, the review and the tests along with the code. Ask students to explain any line the agent wrote.
    
    
    
    
    Your call
    Your policy is yours to set within Brandeis rules. Adjust it whenever it stops serving the learning goal.
